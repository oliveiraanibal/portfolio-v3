In short

The problem

A new loyalty offer took 8 to 12 weeks: manual steps, physical sign-offs and legacy systems that surfaced errors late. Partners waited months to be set up.

What I did

Led the design of the tool on top of a new microservices platform: research with offer teams and approvers, a service blueprint, prototypes, and the offers, partners and permissions interface.

The outcome

Partner onboarding from 100 days to 10 minutes

Reward creation from 2 days to 15 minutes, and 9× fewer errors in offer assignments.

Shell runs loyalty offers in many markets, often with partner brands: fuel discounts, rewards, games. In 2023 Shell asked DEUS.AI to replace the manual processes, legacy systems and siloed teams behind them.

My part

  • Research: stakeholder interviews, task analysis and shadowing.
  • Scenarios, flows and a service blueprint for log-in and offer creation.
  • Interactive prototypes, validated with stakeholders.
  • The interface for offers, partners, users and roles.

The team's part

  • Engineering designed and built the microservices platform: API gateway, gRPC services, CI/CD.
  • Product and Shell stakeholders set priorities and approved each release.
  • Manual, error-prone steps to create offers and onboard partners.
  • Physical sign-offs before an offer could move on.
  • Several legacy systems that didn't share data, so errors appeared late and far from their cause.
  • No shared view of offers and partners across departments.

Markets couldn't react to competitors or seasons, and partners waited months to be set up.
  • Offers sat for weeks waiting for signatures on paper.approval moved into the tool, with an Approver role.
  • Each market runs its own offers.the dashboard is scoped to one market, with a default that opens at login.
  • Many offers repeat with small changes.templates: load one to start, save any offer as one.
  • Errors appeared only after offers reached the loyalty systems.validation before submit, and a Failed status that says which system rejected it.
  • Partners depended on Shell staff for every change.partners get their own sign-in to request access and keep their details current.

Open on the work that needs attention

Why

Three counters (drafts, ready to assign, failed) sit on top of the market dashboard, and each is a filter. The first thing an offer manager sees is their own queue.

Trade-off

Cross-market views take an extra step. Teams rarely needed them, and the default market can be changed with a star.

A form that grows with the offer type

Why

Offer types need very different data. The form starts small and adds only the tabs a type needs. Submit stays disabled, with a line saying why, until every mandatory field is valid.

Trade-off

Complex offers still mean eight tabs. Tabs beat a wizard here: experienced users jump between sections as they edit.

Permissions instead of signatures

Why

Paper sign-offs became four roles per service: administrator, manager, collaborator and approver. Failure is explained where it happens, and changes that ripple into other systems say so first.

Trade-off

A dense permission matrix, shown only to administrators. Making every permission visible was the price of removing paper.

What worked

Designing permissions with the people who sign, not just for them. Paper could only go once the roles matched how responsibility was already divided.

What I'd do differently

Track offer creation end to end. We reported partner onboarding and reward creation, but not the 8–12 week figure we started from, which is the number a business owner remembers.
