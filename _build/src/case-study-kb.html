In short

The problem

A backlog of 50,000+ donated books. Each took a cataloguer 15–20 minutes of searching and typing before it could join the collection.

What I did

Owned the backlog and roadmap for a 6-person team, researched with cataloguers, and designed a capture-and-match flow where AI proposes and the cataloguer decides.

The outcome

40–50% less time per book

98% accuracy in finding duplicates, and a projected 18 months saved on the KB's 2030 roadmap.

The KB collects everything published in the Netherlands, and fills the gaps with donated and second-hand books: the "retro collection". Before a book can join, a cataloguer checks whether the KB already holds that edition in Alma, its cataloguing system, and if not, writes a basic record. DEUS.AI built the RetroTool as a proof of concept that cataloguers tested on real books.

My part

  • Product Owner: backlog, priorities, roadmap and success metrics per phase.
  • Research with cataloguers: observation, interviews, workflow mapping.
  • Lead design: flows, wireframes and the capture and review screens.
  • Stakeholders: the KB's project manager and cataloguers in every review.

The team's part

  • Engineers built the OCR, matching and Alma integrations.
  • The AI team connected Sancus, DEUS's retrieval product, as one of the methods.
  • Cataloguers tested each increment on real books and reported back.

Most of each cataloguer's time went into three jobs: searching Alma for duplicates, often several searches for one book; typing title, author, publisher and year by hand; and writing a record from scratch when the book was new. The backlog grew faster than the team could clear it, and hand-typed records varied in quality.

I watched cataloguers at work, interviewed the most experienced about how they decide two books are the same edition, and tested OCR services, language models and a custom matching model on real title pages with the engineers. Azure Vision read old and unusual typography best, so it became the OCR.

  • The title page and colophon hold almost everything a record needs.capture is two photos under a document camera, and OCR fills eight fields that map to Alma.
  • "Same edition?" is judged field by field, not as a whole.results show a match score for every field, not one yes or no.
  • No single method read every book well.cataloguers can choose the method, and switch when one fails.
  • Cataloguers would only rely on the tool if fixing it was faster than typing.every field stays editable, drafts can be saved, and nothing reaches Alma until a person confirms.

Show why a record matches

Why

Candidate records sit side by side, each with an overall score and a score per field. A 100% title next to a 40% year tells the cataloguer to check the edition before saying yes.

Trade-off

A denser screen than a single "match" answer. Worth it: the decision has to stay with the expert, and the scores make it quick.

Capture first, search second

Why

Photos of the title page and colophon give OCR the best text. One checkbox adds data.bibliotheken.nl and Google Books; one large button searches Alma.

Trade-off

Cataloguers still glance over every field before searching. A wrong field means a wrong search, so a short review beats silent automation.

Let experts choose the method

Why

OCR only, OCR with Google Books, OCR with an LLM through Sancus, or an experimental custom model. We could compare methods on real books, and cataloguers had a fallback for hard ones.

Trade-off

Technical names in a tool for cataloguers. Acceptable for an expert pilot; not for a wider rollout (see reflection).

What worked

Showing the evidence instead of asking for trust. Field-level scores let experts check a match in seconds, which is what made AI suggestions usable for them.

What I'd do differently

Measure inside the tool from day one, so results line up one to one with the targets we set. And name the methods by outcome ("text from photos", "add online sources") with a sensible default.

The LLM method runs on Sancus AI, the retrieval product I worked on at DEUS.
